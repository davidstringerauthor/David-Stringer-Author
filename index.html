The Author

David Stringer

Now at 75, I can see that my life has been a journey to uncover the truth of our shared reality here on Earth.

On this journey I have had to drop many illusions and delusions, studies to become a doctor, training to be an athlete, the urge to be forever young. To search for my identity in places, achievements and loved ones rather than within my own beingness. I have had to strip myself bare to the only thing that could not be negated, our shared human essential spirituality.

Islands of the Heart was born of this journey of discovery. I am not Wolf, but he was real, he did exist and I knew him. He and his own particular journey fascinated me; the people he hurt and the women who loved him fascinated me; the Pacific, and peoples of the Pacific fascinate me; the evil depths and noble summits of human behaviour fascinate me.

I hope I have been able to communicate this in Islands of the Heart. I pray you enjoy the journey, it is all I have to offer.

The Novel

Islands of the Heart

Across the mountains and lakes of New Zealand, the islands of Samoa, and the memories of East Timor, Islands of the Heart follows Wolf as the past refuses to remain buried.

Some journeys begin with a choice. Others begin with a weight something carried so long it has shaped the bones of the person carrying it. For Wolf, the journey north is both an escape and a reckoning, moving through the landscapes he knows and the silences he has never fully broken.

Islands of the Heart moves between the blue-cold beauty of Central Otago, the warmth of Samoan family life, and the shadow of East Timor, a place Wolf has spent years trying not to remember. The novel weaves together Maori, Samoan and Palagi lives, exploring what happens when those worlds collide, break, and sometimes find their way back toward each other.

This is a novel about love and the violence love cannot always prevent. About belonging and the cost of running from it. About the weight of cultural identity, the pull of family, and the question of whether forgiveness is something the living can extend to themselves.

Meet the Characters

Wolf

"The soldier who came home carrying a war he couldn't leave behind."

Wolf is at the centre of the novel, a powerful and complicated man shaped by military experience, family, love and memories he has never fully escaped. His journey takes him across New Zealand and back toward the people and places connected to his past.

He is not a villain, but he is not without fault. He is someone the reader will recognise: a person capable of great loyalty and great damage in the same breath.

"But what really happened in Timor, and can Wolf ever escape the man he became there?"

Tania

"The woman who loves Wolf, but knows there are things he refuses to tell her."

Tania loves Wolf deeply, yet his silences and sudden journey north leave her questioning what she truly knows about the man beside her. She holds the domestic centre of the novel, its quieter grief and its deepest patience.

Her presence reminds us that the people closest to those carrying secrets often carry the weight of those secrets too, without ever being given the words that explain them.

"How much can love survive when the person you love refuses to tell you the truth?"

Lin

"A family bond shaped by love, loss and a past Wolf cannot simply leave behind."

Lin is Wolf's cousin, though their relationship is closer to brother and sister. Half Chinese and half Samoan, she carries profound memories of the past and a painful connection to Benny.

Lin's dual heritage places her at the intersection of the cultural worlds the novel explores. She sees Wolf clearly, perhaps more clearly than anyone and what she knows forms one of the novel's quiet, accumulating pressures.

"What does Lin know about Benny, Bruce and Timor that Wolf has spent years trying to keep buried?"

Pepe

"A mother's love reaching across distance, memory and family."

Pepe represents one of the novel's strongest connections to family, Samoa and belonging. Her relationship with Wolf carries both tenderness and the weight of everything that has happened between them.

In a novel full of people running from their past, Pepe is someone who has stayed who has held the memory of what was and the hope of what might still be.

"Can a mother's love reach a son who has spent years running from his own past?"

Steven

"A father whose ideas about life have shaped Wolf in ways he may not fully understand."

Steven is Wolf's father and an important influence on his ideas about New Zealand, solitude, identity and the meaning of a life. His presence in the novel and the distance between what a father intends to teach and what a son absorbs is one of its quieter threads.

He is a man shaped by his own landscape, his own silences, and the particular New Zealand way of standing apart while still belonging.

"What happens when a father finally has to confront the life he helped create?"

He is a man who has lived fully and seen much and who carries the Samoan understanding that identity is not individual, that belonging is not optional, and that love expressed as toughness is still love.

The Landscape

Meet the Places

In Islands of the Heart, place is not backdrop. It is memory. Each landscape carries the emotional world of the story.

01

New Zealand

"Mountains, lakes, roads and memories."

New Zealand is the novel's bones, its cold clarity and wide open distances giving Wolf space to move, and space to avoid. From Queenstown to Arrowtown, from Lake Wakatipu to the high country beyond Wanaka, the South Island holds its beauty without comfort. It is a landscape that asks who you are and does not wait patiently for an answer.

02

Samoa

"Family, belonging and the pull of the islands."

Where New Zealand is solitude, Samoa is presence. The islands represent what Wolf has run from and what continues to call him, family, warmth, the Samoan understanding that a person is not separate from their people. The Pacific is not merely a setting here. It is a living argument about how a life should be lived.

03

Central Otago

"A landscape that burns its blues, browns and golds into the heart."

Lake Hawea, Wanaka, the high tussock country, Central Otago is one of the most visually dramatic landscapes on earth, and the novel uses it fully. This is where Steven lives, where Wolf grew, and where the particular New Zealand idea of the self-sufficient individual finds its clearest expression. Beautiful, austere and unforgiving.

04

East Timor

"A place Wolf has spent years trying not to remember."

East Timor does not appear on the page in the conventional sense, it arrives through Wolf. Through what he won't say. Through what others know happened there. It is the novel's dark gravity: the event or events that cannot be undone and that explain, without excusing, the man Wolf has become. Its presence is felt in every chapter even when it is not named.

The Book

Islands of the Heart

By David Stringer

Islands of the Heart is a literary novel set across the South Island of New Zealand and the islands of Samoa, moving through the lives of Maori, Samoan and Palagi characters whose worlds collide in moments of love, violence, tenderness and cultural reckoning.

At its centre is Wolf, a man shaped by the land, by family and by the events of East Timor. His journey north becomes an unwilling confrontation with everything he has tried to leave behind: the people who loved him, the people he hurt and the question of whether a man can ever truly outrun his own history.

The novel explores Samoan family life and belonging, the particular isolation of the New Zealand high country, and the way cultural identity, Maori, Samoan, Palagi forms and fractures under the pressures of modern life. It is a novel about the evil depths and the noble summits of human behaviour, written with compassion and unflinching honesty.

For Tania, daylight had often meant crawling out from under the bed where she had slept with her sisters and slipping silently through a house that had frozen in time the acts of the night before a smashed chair and broken beer bottles, congealed pools of vomit and blood; stripped of their power by the coming of the light. Many times had she crept through such a frozen hologram of a violent night, held fast in its glue of odours of beer and cigarettes, the rank stench of dak. Many times, to emerge like a butterfly from her chrysalis, in pretty dress and shoes, to make her way to school. And it had stayed with her, this act of emerging into daylight; for with daylight came freshness and new starts. And belonging. She found Merinda in their favourite café on Hanover Street, enjoying a morning off from long double shifts at the hospital across the road. She looked up as Tania sank into a seat opposite, her brown skin dull from too many hours in aseptic corridors. 'Morning sister. You look like you had a hard night.' 'Probably slept less than you.' Angela Sunde Literary Scholar, Auckland University

Islands of the Heart is a deeply felt and beautifully constructed novel that moves between cultures and landscapes with remarkable assurance. David Stringer writes with an authentic compassion for his characters and an intimate knowledge of the Pacific world they inhabit.

The novel achieves something genuinely difficult: it presents the violence and tenderness of its characters' lives without ever reducing them to type. Wolf is a fully realised human being flawed, searching, capable of harm and of love and the world he moves through feels true in every detail.

Stringer understands the Samoan sense of family and belonging with a depth that gives the novel its emotional centre. And the New Zealand landscape, those extraordinary lakes and mountains is rendered with a lyrical precision that makes it inseparable from the interior lives of the characters who inhabit it.

This is a novel that lingers. A novel that asks difficult questions about identity, cultural belonging, and the possibility of redemption and is honest enough not to answer them too easily. A significant and important work of Pacific literary fiction.
